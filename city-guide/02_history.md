A Brief History

### The Early Inhabitants

Long before European contact, the San Francisco Bay area was home to the Ohlone people, who lived along the coast and the bay for thousands of years. Their settlements were scattered across what is now the city and the surrounding region. The arrival of Spanish missionaries in 1776 — the same year as American independence — brought Mission Dolores, which still stands today as the oldest building in San Francisco and one of the oldest intact structures in California.

### The Mexican Period and American Annexation

Under Spanish and then Mexican rule, the settlement was known as Yerba Buena, named after the wild mint that grew across the hills. It was a small trading post of little consequence until 1848, when the United States seized California following the Mexican-American War. The following year changed everything.

### The Gold Rush

In January 1848, gold was discovered at Sutter's Mill in the Sierra Nevada foothills. Within a year, San Francisco's population exploded from around 1,000 to over 25,000. Ships arrived daily from across the world, many of them later abandoned in the harbor as their crews rushed inland. Some of those ships were later buried under fill as the city expanded its waterfront — and occasionally surface during construction work today.

The Gold Rush built San Francisco's commercial foundations: banks, law firms, newspapers, and merchant houses. It also established the city's instinct for reinvention and its tolerance for eccentricity.

### The 1906 Earthquake and Fire

On April 18, 1906, an estimated magnitude 7.9 earthquake struck the city at dawn. The shaking lasted less than a minute. The fires that followed burned for three days and destroyed roughly 25,000 buildings across 500 city blocks. Over 3,000 people died, and more than half the city's population was left homeless.

The rebuilding that followed was astonishing in its speed. Within a decade the city had largely been reconstructed. The disaster also cemented a certain civic stubbornness that defines San Francisco to this day.

### The Mid-20th Century

The Second World War brought massive activity to the Bay Area, with the Port of San Francisco serving as the main Pacific embarkation point for American troops. The signing of the United Nations Charter took place in San Francisco in 1945, and the city began its long association with international diplomacy and progressive politics.

The postwar years brought the Beat Generation to North Beach, with writers like Jack Kerouac and Allen Ginsberg making the neighborhood's cafes and bars their headquarters. By the 1960s, the Haight-Ashbury district had become the center of the counterculture movement, and the Summer of Love in 1967 drew over 100,000 young people to the city.

### The Tech Eras

Silicon Valley's rise in the 1980s and 1990s began to change the city's demographics and economics. The dot-com boom of the late 1990s brought enormous wealth and a first wave of displacement. After the bust, the city stabilized, only to face a second, more powerful wave beginning around 2010 as companies like Twitter, Uber, and Salesforce made the city their home. The tension between the tech industry and the city's working-class and artistic communities remains one of San Francisco's defining social dynamics.
