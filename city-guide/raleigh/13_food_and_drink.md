# Food & Drink Scene

Raleigh's food scene has undergone a genuine transformation over the past two decades,
evolving from a city better known for drive-through barbecue and chain restaurants into
one of the more interesting dining cities in the American South. The credit belongs
partly to the influx of well-travelled tech workers with money to spend and expectations
formed in bigger cities, partly to a generation of North Carolina chefs who trained in New
York and came home, and partly to the extraordinary agricultural richness of the North
Carolina piedmont, which provides pork, sweet potatoes, sweet corn, muscadine grapes,
and seafood within a few hours' drive of the city.

**Barbecue** is still the baseline. North Carolina has its own barbecue tradition that
divides sharply between the eastern vinegar-and-whole-hog style and the western
Lexington-style (shoulders, red slaw, tomato-tinged sauce). Raleigh sits near the
geographic divide and offers both. The Pit on Dawson Street is the most famous
downtown option, but locals argue that the best 'cue is found in the surrounding counties
at family-owned joints that haven't changed since the 1970s.

**Contemporary dining**: Crawford and Son (North Person Street) has been near the top
of every "best Raleigh restaurant" list since its opening and consistently delivers
refined New American cooking built on local ingredients. Bida Manda (Dawson Street)
introduced Laotian cuisine to Raleigh with remarkable success. Boulted Bread (Seaboard
Station) is the city's best bakery and one of the better ones in the South. Poole's
Diner (McDowell Street) is the original downtown revival restaurant, a 1950s diner
reimagined by chef Ashley Christensen into something entirely contemporary.

**Ramen and noodles**: Raleigh has developed an unexpectedly strong ramen scene. Nofo
at the Pig and Sono Japanese Cuisine are well-regarded; the more recent arrivals include
Waraji (longstanding local institution on Falls of Neuse Road) and several newer shops
in the downtown core.

**Craft beer**: Raleigh has a density of craft breweries that would be remarkable for a
city twice its size. Trophy Brewing (two locations, including one in the Warehouse
District) and Lynnwood Brewing Concern are strong locals. Big Boss Brewing (west side)
was one of the early Triangle craft breweries and remains good. The craft cocktail scene
has grown alongside the beer scene; Foundation Bar (Glenwood South) and Death & Taxes
(Fayetteville Street) are the best known.

**Coffee**: Jubala Coffee (Person Street, with locations across the city) is the local
favourite, built on a loyalty from NC State and the creative class. Raleigh Times Bar
(Hargett Street) has good espresso embedded in a proper bar setting.

**Farmers' markets**: The State Farmers' Market on Western Boulevard is one of the largest
in the Southeast, open every day of the year, and worth a visit for the sheer variety of
North Carolina produce, meats, and preserves.

For workshop participants, the most useful food cluster is the downtown core between
Moore Square and the Warehouse District — a fifteen-minute walk can connect you to a
dozen genuinely good options at every price point.
