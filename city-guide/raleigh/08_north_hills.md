# North Hills

North Hills is Raleigh's primary mid-century commercial hub, located about four miles
north of downtown along the Six Forks Road corridor, roughly centred on the intersection
of Six Forks and North Hills Drive near the I-440 Beltline. It is not the kind of place
you would call a neighbourhood in the traditional sense — it is more a node of commercial
and residential activity — but it is one of the more significant parts of Raleigh's
suburban geography, and knowing what it is helps you understand how the city is structured.

The original North Hills Shopping Center opened in the early 1960s, one of the first
enclosed malls in North Carolina. Like many mid-century malls, it struggled and
contracted as retail patterns shifted, but unlike most it has been substantially
redeveloped in the 2000s and 2010s into a mixed-use district of apartments, office towers,
hotels, a Whole Foods, and a renovated outdoor shopping district with restaurants and
shops occupying former mall anchor spaces.

**What North Hills is today** is essentially an upmarket live-work-shop district built
on the skeleton of a suburban mall. It has a Cinébistro (dine-in cinema), a range of
chain and independent restaurants, a Marriott and a Sheraton, and a significant number
of apartments and condominiums in mid-rise towers that have gone up since 2015.

**The surrounding residential neighbourhoods** — North Hills proper, Quail Hollow,
Bedford — are large-lot single-family suburbs from the 1960s through 1980s, populated
mainly by established Raleigh families and professionals. They are quiet and pleasant and
unremarkable in the way that successful mid-century suburbs tend to be.

**Getting to North Hills** from downtown requires a car, rideshare, or the GoRaleigh
Route 5 (Six Forks Road), which runs north from downtown to the North Hills area in about
twenty-five minutes. There is no easy bike route from downtown. Most visitors to North
Hills drive.

**For workshop context**: North Hills matters mainly as a reference point for understanding
how Raleigh's employment and retail geography works. The inside-the-Beltline corridor
along Six Forks Road, which runs north from downtown through the North Hills area to
Creedmoor Road, is one of the city's most economically productive strips — a reminder that
Raleigh's wealth is not concentrated in the downtown core the way it is in older cities,
but distributed across multiple nodes connected by arterial roads.

The innovation economy that drives Research Triangle Park spreads its residential effects
northward into the Six Forks corridor. Many RTP employees live in North Raleigh and
commute south on Six Forks or US-1 to get to their campuses. Understanding this flow
helps explain why Raleigh's morning rush-hour traffic runs north-to-south rather than the
reverse, and why the city's transit planners face particular challenges with a population
distributed across wide suburban areas rather than concentrated near transit corridors.
