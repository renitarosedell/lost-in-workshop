# History of Raleigh

Raleigh is one of the few American cities that exists entirely by design. In 1788, after
years of the state legislature meeting in New Bern, Hillsborough, Halifax, and other towns,
North Carolina's General Assembly decided that the state needed a permanent, purpose-built
capital. A commission was appointed to find a site near the geographic centre of the state.

The commissioners chose a tract of land owned by Joel Lane, a local landowner and
Revolutionary War veteran, in the hilly piedmont between the Neuse and Cape Fear river
basins. The state purchased 1,000 acres from Lane for £1,378 in 1792. A town plan was
drawn up by William Christmas, a surveyor, who laid out a grid of streets with four public
squares at the corners of a central larger square to be occupied by the State House. The
city was named after Sir Walter Raleigh, the Elizabethan explorer who had sponsored the
failed Roanoke colony on the North Carolina coast in the 1580s.

**The nineteenth century** saw Raleigh grow slowly. The State House burned in 1831 and
was replaced by the current State Capitol, completed in 1840 — a handsome Greek Revival
building in the style of the age. NC State University was founded in 1887 as a land-grant
institution, and Shaw University (1865) and St. Augustine's University (1867) were
established to educate formerly enslaved people in the years after the Civil War.

Raleigh was occupied by Union forces in April 1865 without significant fighting — the
city's mayor surrendered to General Sherman's army after Confederate General Joseph
Johnston moved his troops southwest. This spared Raleigh the destruction that Columbia,
South Carolina suffered, and the downtown building stock from the 1840s through 1900s
is consequently well preserved.

**The twentieth century**: The Great Depression was severe in a city whose economy depended
on state government employment and tobacco processing. Recovery came through New Deal
federal spending and the slow growth of the university sector. Research Triangle Park's
1959 founding changed everything: the influx of IBM in 1965 signalled that the Park would
become a major employer, and the population of the Triangle began the long upward arc
that continues today.

**Civil rights in Raleigh**: The Woolworth's sit-in movement reached Raleigh in 1960,
with NC State and Shaw University students staging lunch counter protests. The city
desegregated its public schools in the late 1960s under court order. Today Raleigh has
a majority-minority population and significant African American political representation.

**Contemporary Raleigh**: From 2000 to 2020, Raleigh was one of the fastest-growing cities
in the United States, nearly doubling its population. The growth has brought the urban
revival of downtown, the proliferation of craft breweries and restaurants, the construction
of a new convention centre and performing arts complex, and the ongoing debates about
housing affordability, transportation investment, and the preservation of historic
neighbourhoods that accompany rapid growth everywhere.
